We know a great deal about what immigration does to European elections. Two
decades of research have established that attitudes towards newcomers predict
vote choice, welfare preferences and Euroscepticism, and that radical-right
parties have built much of their success on the issue.

We know almost nothing about the other direction. Yet for roughly half the
European Union — the eastern and southern periphery — the politically salient
migration story is not who is arriving. It is who is leaving, and not coming
back.

## A question nobody had the data for

Free movement is a founding promise of the EU and remains genuinely popular. But
it has two faces, and they are politicised in different places. In receiving
countries, free movement gets attacked as immigration. In sending countries,
politicians increasingly attack it as *emigration* — as the slow draining of a
country's young, educated and employable, usually bundled with anxieties about
low fertility and ageing.

The rhetoric is not subtle. Viktor Orbán has told the Hungarian parliament that
hundreds of thousands of adults of childbearing age left because they could not
build a future at home. Giorgia Meloni made much the same argument in Italy
before becoming prime minister. In Portugal, Spain, Greece and Romania,
opposition parties of very different stripes have used departure figures as an
indictment of whoever is in office.

What nobody had established was whether any of this reaches voters. Most existing
work infers the political effect of emigration from *regional* data — depopulation
rates against election results — which cannot distinguish between two very
different mechanisms: the people who leave were different voters, or the people
who stay became angrier.

We used individual-level survey data instead. The source is an original survey
fielded by Gallup in the summer of 2021 across fifteen European countries as part
of the SOLID project, funded by an ERC Synergy Grant. Around 1,000 respondents
per country. We restricted the analysis to the nine countries where emigration is
actually a live concern: Greece, Hungary, Ireland, Italy, Latvia, Poland,
Portugal, Romania and Spain.

The key question was direct. Thinking about freedom of movement in the EU, how
concerned are you about your fellow citizens emigrating to other EU countries?
Zero to ten.

## Concern moves votes, but not where you would guess

Our first expectation was the intuitive one: worry about emigration should push
voters towards the radical flanks, left and right, since mainstream parties
generally defend free movement.

That is not what the pooled data show. Across the nine countries, higher concern
about emigration is significantly associated with *not* voting for the
conservative party family. It shows no average association with voting radical
right. And it is not simply generalised disaffection either — worried voters are
no more likely to abstain or spoil their ballot than anyone else.

The conservative result makes sense once you sit with it. Conservative parties in
these countries have generally championed market liberalisation, austerity and
privatisation, the policies most plausibly blamed for the brain drain in the first
place. Voters who feel left behind by departure punish the parties they associate
with the departure.

## Trust is the mechanism

The finding I find most interesting concerns incumbents, and it only appears when
you stop treating political trust as a nuisance variable.

Taken on its own, concern about emigration has no relationship with voting for
the parties in government. Add political trust to the model and a significant
negative association appears. Add the interaction between the two, and the
picture resolves: **concern about emigration damages incumbents only among voters
who trust institutions.**

Trust normally works as a resource for whoever is in power — trusting citizens
support the government. What emigration concern does is spend that resource down.
Among high-trust voters, rising worry about emigration steadily erodes the
probability of supporting the incumbent; among low-trust voters, there was not
much support there to lose. The pattern is sharpest in Hungary, Latvia, Romania
and Spain.

This is a specific, and rather demanding, kind of accountability. It requires
voters who believe governments can act. Cynics do not punish anyone.

## The country-by-country picture is a mess, productively

Disaggregating by country, the radical right does benefit — in Greece (Golden
Dawn), Poland (Kukiz'15), Ireland (Aontú) and Hungary (Jobbik and Mi Hazánk). So
the standard story is not wrong, just partial.

But emigration concern also helps SYRIZA in Greece, Lewica in Poland, the Five
Star Movement in Italy, and a socialist-led alliance in Hungary. And in Hungary it
significantly *hurts* Fidesz — a radical-right party that has governed since 2010
and presided over a great deal of emigration.

The pattern underneath the noise is not ideological. It is positional. Emigration
concern never once helped a large centrist party with mass appeal. It always
helped somebody small, niche and out of office.

That suggests emigration behaves less like an ideological issue with a natural
owner and more like an available grievance — a stick that any party outside
government can pick up, provided the local conditions are right. Which parties
pick it up, and whether it sticks, has to be established case by case.
