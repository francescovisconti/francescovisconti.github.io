> I study what citizens want from the European Union — and what happens to
> politics when those wants collide. Five keywords cover most of it: **European
> integration**, **free movement**, **solidarity**, **climate change**, and
> **public opinion**. I work with comparative survey data, survey experiments,
> and quantitative text analysis.

## European solidarity

Who is willing to share risks with citizens of other member states, and under
what conditions? I have worked on both halves of that question: what drives
support for supranational solidarity, and what that support does to political
competition once it enters the electoral arena.

The short answer to the second half is that it matters more than we assumed.
Preferences over European solidarity do not simply map onto the old left-right
axis — they structure a dimension of their own, and they move votes. In the 2019
European Parliament elections, positions on solidarity predicted vote choice
independently of the standard cleavages
([*European Union Politics*](https://doi.org/10.1177/14651165211035054)), and
after the polycrisis this dimension has become a stable feature of EU political
competition
([*Journal of Common Market Studies*](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcms.13651)).

I have also looked at what citizens actually know about Europe's redistributive
machinery: awareness of Cohesion Funds turns out to be unevenly distributed in
ways that track individual power resources, with consequences for EU support
([*Journal of European Social Policy*](https://doi.org/10.1177/09589287231210727)).

## Free movement and its two faces

Free movement is usually studied from the receiving end. I have worked on both
ends, because they generate different politics.

**Entering.** Transnational experience — having lived, worked, studied, or built
ties across borders — changes people. It raises engagement with European politics
([*Electoral Studies*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.electstud.2021.102383)) and
shifts voting behaviour
([*Journal of Elections, Public Opinion and Parties*](https://doi.org/10.1080/17457289.2023.2241817)).
But the effect on solidarity is not the uncomplicated cosmopolitan story one
might expect
([*Journal of European Public Policy*](https://doi.org/10.1080/13501763.2022.2153155)).

**Leaving.** The countries people leave have a politics of their own, and it is
badly understudied. I have examined how concern about emigration relates to
demand for national and European social investment
([*International Migration Review*](https://doi.org/10.1177/01979183231185096)),
how it interacts with political trust, and how it translates into vote choice
once parties decide to politicise it
([*Government & Opposition*](https://doi.org/10.1017/gov.2025.10031)).

## Climate politics

My current work asks how climate change becomes a partisan issue and what
citizens are prepared to give up for the transition.

The trade-offs are the interesting part. Support for the green transition is not
a single preference: growth, ecological sustainability, and equity pull in
different directions, and citizens resolve the tension in patterned ways
([*European Journal of Political Research*](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1475676526100784)).
The Russian invasion of Ukraine offered a natural test of how quickly energy and
compensation preferences reorganise under pressure
([*Journal of European Public Policy*](https://doi.org/10.1080/13501763.2023.2217228)).

On the Italian case, I co-edited a special issue on the politicisation of climate
change ([*Italian Political Science*](https://italianpoliticalscience.com/index.php/ips/issue/view/34/33)),
and I am currently working on a cross-national survey — which I helped design —
measuring support for eco-social policies through a survey experiment.

## Earlier work: representation and agendas

Before turning to the EU, I worked on the domestic side of the same question:
whether the priorities of the public reach the legislative agenda. That research
looked at policy priorities in the Italian parliament
([*Italian Political Science Review*](https://doi.org/10.1017/ipo.2018.4)) and at
government agenda-setting in coalition governments
([*Rivista Italiana di Politiche Pubbliche*](https://doi.org/10.1483/87214)).
